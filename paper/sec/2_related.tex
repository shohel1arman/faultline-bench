\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hallucination and grounding.}
Object hallucination was first measured in captioning~\cite{rohrbach2018object} and later by polling VLMs about object presence~\cite{li2023pope}. HallusionBench~\cite{guan2024hallusionbench} combines visual illusions with language priors, and Tong \etal~\cite{tong2024eyes} show that VLMs fail on image pairs their vision encoders cannot tell apart. These benchmarks ask whether a model's own output is grounded; \name instead gives the model a wrong input and asks whether it notices, resists and explains it.

\paragraph{Foils and compositional descriptions.}
FOIL~\cite{shekhar2017foil} replaces one word in a COCO caption and asks models to detect and locate it, which is close to our detection and span probes. VALSE~\cite{parcalabescu2022valse}, Winoground~\cite{thrush2022winoground}, ARO~\cite{yuksekgonul2023bags} and SugarCrepe~\cite{hsieh2023sugarcrepe} test whether models prefer a correct caption over a minimally changed one. They score a choice between captions; we additionally ask a question whose answer depends on which input the model trusts, grade each edit by difficulty, and compare the model's report of the error with its own answers.

\paragraph{Text--image conflicts in VLMs.}
Several recent studies give VLMs text that contradicts the image. Deng \etal~\cite{deng2025words} find that VLMs often follow inconsistent text over visual evidence, which they call blind faith in text. Mixed Signals~\cite{pezeshkpour2025mixed} shows that the preferred modality depends on task difficulty and model size, MC$^2$~\cite{zhang2025mc2} measures and steers modality preference, persuasive text that contradicts the image sharply lowers accuracy~\cite{zhang2026images}, and V-FAT~\cite{wang2026vfat} grades items by the source of bias (corpus prior, misleading prompt, or both). These works measure which input the answer follows, but not whether the model notices the conflict. CLASH~\cite{popordanoska2025clash} and CrossCheck-Bench~\cite{tian2026crosscheck} target contradiction detection, and GAVEL~\cite{gao2026gavel} asks models to verify a caption, explain the error and box the image evidence. Related studies cast the conflict as image versus prior knowledge~\cite{chen2026cdhbench,wang2026dobench} or place it inside retrieved evidence~\cite{catapang2026cmcbench}. Mechanistic work finds that conflict detection and resolution use different internal signals~\cite{nguyen2025challenges} and that late layers can override a visually correct answer with the text~\cite{li2026mllms}. \name differs in measuring detection, resolution and attribution on the same single-span edits, grading each edit by perceptual difficulty, adding perception controls that separate seeing the fact from trusting it, and following one model family from 2B to 32B. Three contemporaneous works are complementary: answers shift toward whichever evidence comes last~\cite{li2026noncommutativity}, which bears on our delayed-question control; explanations of VLMs often fail counterfactual edits of the concepts they cite~\cite{ding2026edct}; and a cue-combination model accounts for modality preference~\cite{barua2026bayesian}.

\paragraph{Knowledge conflicts in language models.}
For text-only models, conflicts between the context and the model's parametric knowledge are well studied~\cite{longpre2021entity,xie2024adaptive,xu2024knowledge}. Our setting differs in that both sources are inputs, and the model can check one against the other.

\paragraph{Faithfulness of self-reports.}
Explanations produced by language models need not reflect what drives their answers~\cite{turpin2023language,lanham2023measuring}, and models tend to agree with what a user asserts~\cite{sharma2024towards}. Our attribution and verbalize probes test this for multimodal conflicts: whether a model's report of the error agrees with its own detection answer, and whether writing the report changes the answer it then gives.
